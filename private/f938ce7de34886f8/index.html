Verbalized Confidence vs. Logit-Expected Confidence

The clean question

You ask the model "rate your confidence 0–100" and it emits some number $c$. At that emission position, the model also has a full distribution $p(\text{token} \mid \text{context})$ over what it could have said. Define

$$c_E = \sum_k p_k \cdot v_k$$

where $v_k$ is the numeric value of token $k$. The subscript $E$ stands for expectation: $c_E = \mathbb{E}[v]$ is the mean of the model's own distribution over confidence numbers, whereas $c$ is a single sample drawn from it. Question: does $c$ correlate with $c_E$?

As a toy example, suppose the numeric mass at the emission position is:

token$p_k$$v_k$
"90"0.690
"85"0.385
"95"0.195

then the model emits $c = 90$ (the most likely draw), but $c_E = 0.6(90) + 0.3(85) + 0.1(95) = 89.0$. Same position, same forward pass: $c$ is what came out, $c_E$ is what it would average to.

The three rows are just for intuition. The real distribution is a heavy-tailed softmax over the whole vocabulary, so in practice we keep the numeric tokens (from the top-K logprobs the API returns) and renormalize them to sum to one before taking the expectation.

What we'd actually expect to see

Three regimes, each interesting:

Regime 1: Near-deterministic emission ($H_c \approx 0$)

The distribution is a delta on one token. $c = c_E$ trivially, correlation = 1.0, no information beyond the sample. This is what greedy decoding or temperature=0 gives you. Likely common on strong instruction-tuned models that have been RLHF'd to emit "85" or "95" confidently.

Regime 2: Peaked but stochastic

The distribution puts ~70% on "85", ~20% on "90", ~10% on "80". Sampled $c$ is usually 85, sometimes 80 or 90. $c_E \approx 84.5$, stable across samples. Per-instance $\mathrm{corr}(c, c_E)$ across $n$ samples is moderate; across instances the correlation between mean sampled $c$ and $c_E$ is very high.

Regime 3: Diffuse / bimodal

Distribution is spread over $\{30, 50, 80, 95\}$ or has two modes. $c$ jumps around per sample; $c_E$ is some weighted average that may not even be in the support. Here $c$ and $c_E$ diverge — and that divergence is the signal.

So the right framing isn't "are they correlated" (yes, trivially, in regimes 1–2) but "where do they diverge, and does the divergence carry information?"

What this does and doesn't measure

One caveat to be explicit about: the distribution over confidence tokens is a distribution about a report, not about the task. $c_E$ and $H_c$ describe how sure the model is about what number to say, not how sure it is about the answer. The two come apart:

  • Certain the answer is "B" but fuzzy about whether to call it 85 or 90 → high $H_c$, yet low task uncertainty.
  • Torn between two answers but trained to always stamp "95" → low $H_c$, yet high task uncertainty.

So $H_c$ and $\Delta = c - c_E$ are signals of the internal consistency of the self-report — whether the spoken number is faithful to the model's own token distribution. This is a question about the model's relationship to itself; it needs no ground truth and says nothing, on its own, about whether the answer is correct. That is a separate axis we deliberately set aside here.

Concrete experiment to run

Pick one open-weight model (Llama-3.3-70B is convenient; vLLM exposes top-k logprobs cleanly), one benchmark with verbalized confidence in the prompt template, ~500 instances. Use a temperature where the confidence distribution actually moves — on near-greedy decoding many RLHF'd models mode-collapse onto a handful of values ("90", "95", "100") and the whole thing flattens into Regime 1, where there is nothing to study (see caveat below).

For each instance:

  1. Run the prompt, get the trace + confidence emission.
  2. At the confidence position, capture top-20 logprobs.
  3. Filter to numeric continuations, renormalize, compute $c_E$.
  4. Record $(c, c_E, H_c)$.

Then plot:

  • Scatter of $c$ vs. $c_E$: across all instances. Pearson $r$ and the residual structure.
  • Histogram of $\Delta = c - c_E$: is it centered at zero, or systematically biased (e.g., model emits 95 but its distribution averages to 80 — overconfident sampling)?
  • $H_c$ vs. $|\Delta|$: high entropy positions should be where $c$ and $c_E$ disagree most.

What the answer tells you

  • $r \approx 1.0$, $\Delta \approx 0$: verbalized confidence is just a deterministic readout of the underlying distribution. Logits add nothing the sampled number doesn't already give you. (Sampling once is enough.)
  • $r$ high, $\Delta$ biased: there's a systematic gap between what the model would "average" to and what it emits. This is the most interesting outcome — it says the sampling distribution has a mode the model's expectation disagrees with, which is a kind of self-inconsistency you can detect from one forward pass.
  • $r$ moderate, $H_c$ predicts $|\Delta|$: $H_c$ is a useful per-instance signal of self-report inconsistency — the model's uncertainty about what to say shows up as distributional spread, not just a low number.

Why this is useful

The verbalized number is the only confidence signal most people ever read off a model — they sample once, parse "90", and trust it. This investigation asks whether that single token is a faithful summary of the distribution it was drawn from. The payoff is independent of any correctness question:

  • It tells you whether one sample is enough. If $c \approx c_E$ everywhere (Regime 1–2), the verbalized number already is the distribution's center — no need to sample $n$ times or pull logits; one greedy decode suffices. If they diverge, the single sample is throwing away information the logits hold.
  • $\Delta = c - c_E$ is a free self-inconsistency flag. When the model emits a number its own distribution doesn't support, that is detectable from one forward pass, with no labels and no extra calls. It marks the instances where the spoken confidence is least trustworthy as a summary.
  • $c_E$ is a denoised confidence. The expectation is continuous and averages out sampling noise, so even when the emitted token is coarse ("85" or "90" and nothing between), $c_E$ recovers a finer-grained value — a better quantity to feed downstream than the raw token.
  • $H_c$ surfaces hidden indecision. A model can stamp a confident "90" while its distribution is actually split across $\{70, 90\}$; $H_c$ exposes that the number was a near-tie the sampler happened to break one way.

Modeling $c$ vs. $c_E$ when the numbers concentrate

A real concern: verbalized confidences tend to pile up on a few round values — 80, 90, 95, 100. Two things follow, and both are tractable.

First, the concentration is itself the finding. If $c$ takes only a handful of values, the model has, in effect, a coarse confidence vocabulary. The question becomes: within each emitted value, how does $c_E$ behave? Group the instances by the emitted token and look at the conditional distribution of $c_E$:

emitted $c$mean $c_E$spread of $c_E$reading
90~0.90tight"90" is a faithful, decisive readout
90~0.78wide"90" is a rounding-up of a softer, variable belief

If the second row is what you see, the model is discretizing a continuous internal confidence onto round numbers — and $c_E$ is recovering the continuous quantity that "90" was rounding.

Second, you can model the map explicitly. Treat the emitted token as a quantized, possibly biased function of the underlying expectation and fit it:

  • Rounding / quantization model: $c = \mathrm{round}_{\mathcal{G}}(c_E + b)$, where $\mathcal{G} = \{0, 50, 80, 90, 95, 100\}$ is the grid the model actually uses and $b$ is a bias. Fitting $\mathcal{G}$ and $b$ tells you which round numbers the model snaps to and whether it systematically rounds up.
  • Monotone regression $c = g(c_E)$: fit isotonic or a simple curve. A staircase-shaped $g$ confirms quantization; its step locations are the model's confidence vocabulary. The residual $c - g(c_E)$ is the part of the spoken number not explained by the distribution mean — sampling noise plus whatever idiosyncrasy the verbalizer adds.
  • Ordinal model: treat the few values as ordered categories and fit an ordered-logit with $c_E$ (and $H_c$) as predictors. The cutpoints recover the $c_E$ thresholds at which the model switches from saying "80" to "90".

In all three, the object of study is the deterministic-plus-noise relationship between a coarse spoken token and a continuous internal expectation. Concentration doesn't break the analysis — it turns it into a clean quantization question, where $c_E$ is the latent value and $c$ is its rounded report.

The tokenization wrinkle (real but tractable)

"95" might be one token in some tokenizers, "9" + "5" in others, " 95" with a leading space in others. Three options:

  1. Constrain the prompt: ask for confidence as a single token from $\{$"0", "5", "10", ..., "100"$\}$. Now the position is unambiguous.
  2. Marginalize: at position $t$, for each top-K token, complete greedily (or beam-search) until you hit a non-numeric token, parse the number, weight by $p_t(\text{token})$. Approximate but works for any tokenizer.
  3. Digit-by-digit logits: capture $p(d_1)$, $p(d_2 \mid d_1)$, etc. and reconstruct the full distribution over numeric strings. Most faithful, most engineering.

For a first pass, option 1 gets you a clean answer in an afternoon.
